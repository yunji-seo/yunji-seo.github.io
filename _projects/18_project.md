_This project was completed as part of "Community Planning and Public Policy for Disability" course at University of California, Berkeley._

### __Overview__

San Francisco is a city of innovation, yet it faces a severe housing crisis that disproportionately affects people with disabilities. This project examines **The Kelsey Civic Center**, a mixed-income affordable housing development, to understand how urban planning can move beyond physical compliance to foster genuine social inclusion. By analyzing its design principles, stakeholder engagement, and policy framework, this study highlights a scalable model for "Cities for All."

Historically, housing for people with disabilities has been synonymous with exclusion—either through segregation in institutional facilities or physical inaccessibility in the general market.
* **Physical Exclusion:** Lack of elevators, accessible bathrooms, and sensory-friendly features.
* **Structural Exclusion:** Complex application systems and unaffordable rents.
* **Social Exclusion:** Isolation from the broader community.

Despite 26% of the U.S. population having a disability, less than 6% of housing stock is accessible. This structural gap necessitates a paradigm shift in how we conceive urban housing.
<br>

### __Intervention: The Kelsey Civic Center__

Located directly across from San Francisco City Hall, The Kelsey Civic Center is not a facility, but a vibrant community of 112 affordable homes.

* **Inclusivity Target:** 25% of units are reserved for people with disabilities, while the rest are for low-income workers and families. This creates a true "mixed-ability" community rather than a segregated silo.
* **Affordability:** Serving households earning 20-80% of the Area Median Income (AMI).
* **Location:** Its central location ensures residents have direct access to public transit, civic services, and cultural institutions, reinforcing their "Right to the City."

<br>

### __Design & Program Strategies__

The project employs **Universal Design** principles, ensuring that spaces are usable by everyone, regardless of ability or age.

**1. Physical Accessibility**
* Removable kitchen and bathroom cabinets for wheelchair users.
* Dimmable lighting for residents with sensory sensitivities.
* Color-coded floors to aid navigation for those with cognitive disabilities.

**2. Social Inclusion Programs**
* **Inclusion Concierge:** A dedicated role to connect residents with services and facilitate community bonding.
* **Disability Cultural Community Center:** Located on the ground floor, this is the nation’s first publicly funded cultural space led by and for people with disabilities, anchoring the building as a civic hub.
<br>

### __Policy Insights & Conclusion__

The success of The Kelsey Civic Center offers key lessons for urban planners and policymakers:

* **Participation:** The design process directly involved the disability community, ensuring solutions met real-life needs (e.g., plain-language leases).
* **Cross-Sector Leadership:** It required a robust partnership between non-profits (The Kelsey), developers (Mercy Housing), and city/state agencies.
* **Integration over Segregation:** Unlike traditional models, it proves that accessible housing should be integrated into dense, lively neighborhoods.

**"True inclusion starts when accessibility is built into the heart of our cities—not added after the fact."**
This project demonstrates that inclusive housing is not just a welfare issue but a fundamental component of sustainable, diverse, and vibrant cities.
