### __Overview__

Our team produced a short documentary-style video titled Bad Place Vlog: Sewoon Elevated Walkway to analyze why the elevated pedestrian network connecting the Sewoon Megacomplex has struggled to attract users. Using field observation, archival footage, interviews with merchants, and data visualization, we examined the walkway’s urban design context, its intended purpose, and the reasons behind its limited success. The project combined spatial analysis with on-site documentation to better understand how urban design decisions shape human behavior and place performance.
<br>

### __Background: The Sewoon Megacomplex__

Built between 1967 and 1972, Sewoon Sangga is Korea’s first mixed-use megastructure, consisting of Sewoon, Cheonggye, Daerim, Sampung, PJ, Inhyeon, and Jinyang buildings.
In the 1970s and 80s, the area thrived as a dense electronics market, drawing large crowds.
However, as distribution structures changed and Yongsan Electronics Market emerged, Sewoon rapidly declined.

Until 2015, each building had fragmented or missing elevated walkways. After the area was selected as a Seoul Urban Regeneration Zone, the city built a continuous 1 km north–south elevated pedestrian connection from Sewoon to Jinyang in an effort to attract visitors, improve walkability, and link Jongmyo, Euljiro and Chungmuro.
<br>

### __Analysis__

#### __1. Place Characteristics and Initial Observations__

Despite its central location—300 meters from Jongno 3-ga Station—the elevated walkway felt noticeably quieter than the ground level. Filming at 6:45 p.m. on a Friday, when the area is usually busy, revealed that the elevated level was almost empty while the street below remained active.

This raised a primary question:
__Why is the elevated walkway significantly less used than expected?__
<br>

#### __2. Failure Factor 1: Lack of Active Frontage__

Our video showed that segments with active uses—such as the café cluster inside Daerim Sangga—attracted more people.
However, large portions of the walkway run along blank building walls or industrial back façades, offering no visual interest or reason to remain.

From urban design theory, people prefer environments where they can interact with surroundings—what is called active frontage. Places lacking this tend to feel empty and unsafe, reducing pedestrian activity.

In the current condition, foot traffic from active zones does not spill over to adjacent buildings because the walkway provides no meaningful programs or attractions.
<br>

#### __3. Failure Factor 2: Weak Pedestrian Friendliness and Poor Visibility__

A key principle in urban design is natural surveillance—people feel safer when they can see and be seen.

However:
- From the ground level, it is often difficult to see what is happening on the elevated walkway.
- Certain sections consist of metal grates and dim corridors, which users described as “scary.”
- Some elevated segments are used as storage, further deteriorating the pedestrian environment.

Multiple merchants told us they avoid the walkway when commuting because it feels unsafe.
<br>

#### __4. Failure Factor 3: Misaligned Demand and Flow Patterns__

We analyzed Seoul’s administrative-dong trip purpose data and visualized pedestrian movement. The results showed:
- __East–west movement (Euljiro–Chungmuro axis)__ is significantly stronger than
- __North–south movement (Jongmyo–Sewoon–Chungmuro axis)__.

In central Jongno–Jung-gu, north–south flows are 40% lower than east–west flows, and much of the north–south volume is attributable to Sejong-daero—meaning Sewoon’s expected user base was overestimated.

Actual pedestrian counts collected from October to March also showed usage at __only 5–17% of the city’s 2017 projections.__

This indicates that the walkway was built on an assumption of demand that does not align with observed movement patterns.
<br>

#### __5. Merchant Perspectives__

Interviews revealed that the walkway did not deliver the intended benefits:
- No significant increase in foot traffic
- Higher vacancies in surrounding buildings
- Difficult fire-engine access due to structural changes
- Reduced visibility of ground-level businesses

One merchant, who has been protesting for six years, explained that the walkway has not created meaningful interactions between buildings and instead imposed additional burdens on existing tenants.
<br>

### __Results__

__Findings__

The Sewoon elevated walkway illustrates how urban design interventions fail when they do not align with:
1. Human-scale spatial experience
2. Active and engaging street-level uses
3. Visibility and safety principles
4. Evidence-based pedestrian demand
5. Local community needs and merchant input

The walkway offers impressive views of the city, but design and contextual misalignments have prevented it from becoming a successful public space.
<br>

### __Conclusion__

Our video highlights that successful urban places require more than physical connectivity—they need programs, visibility, social interaction, and realistic assessments of behavioral patterns.
Future improvements must integrate scientific evaluation, user research and merchant feedback to create a walkway that people naturally choose to use.
