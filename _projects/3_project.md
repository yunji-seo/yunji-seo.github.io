# __Team Reflection on _What’s Wrong with Apartments?___


### __Overview__

Our team examined how Korea’s apartment-centered redevelopment model shapes the physical and social landscape of its cities. By looking at infrastructure projects, redevelopment patterns, and everyday urban forms, we explored the values driving large-scale construction and the inevitable trade-offs. Our goal was not to argue that apartments are inherently problematic, but to understand what the built environment reveals about the forces shaping Korean cities and who they truly serve.
<br>

### __Background__

We began by challenging the notion that “artificial” or heavily engineered environments are automatically negative. Examples such as hydropower facilities and the Cheonggyecheon restoration (page 6) demonstrated that deliberate interventions can improve public space and support urban life. However, we also found clear evidence that infrastructure carries significant risks and costs, from tunnel construction issues to worker fatalities during major bridge projects (pages 12–14). These cases prompted us to reflect on which values, whether efficiency, convenience, safety, or equity, are prioritized during urban development.
<br>

### __Urban Form and Density__

To understand how different development choices shape land use, we created diagrams comparing dispersed low-density housing with high-density apartment clusters (pages 16–20). Through this comparison, we discussed how low-density layouts require more land and fragmented infrastructure, whereas high-density redevelopment concentrates residents but often results in uniform, monotonous landscapes. This exercise helped us evaluate how density, diversity, and human scale interact within the design of Korean cities.
<br>

### __Redevelopment and Its Implications__

We then analyzed the mechanics of redevelopment in Korea. Our review of examples on pages 21–25 highlighted that large-scale projects are frequently led by private stakeholders, tending to prioritize efficiency and economic return. While this model increases housing supply and can upgrade local infrastructure, it also accelerates land value appreciation and residential displacement. The charts on page 25 illustrate how redevelopment is closely tied to rising housing prices, often exacerbating social inequalities and limiting who can afford to remain in newly developed areas.
<br>

### __Toward Better Cities__

We concluded that creating better cities requires more than simply erecting new structures. Cities should reflect values such as safety, environmental responsibility, inclusiveness, and long-term resilience. We discussed the need for redevelopment to consider not just physical upgrades, but also impacts on communities, ecological systems, and local identity.

Instead of asking “What’s wrong with apartments?”, we reframed the question as “What kind of urban environment should we aim for?” Through this project, we sought to encourage a more balanced perspective on efficiency, social equity, and environmental quality in urban planning.

# __Personal Reflection on _What’s Wrong with Apartments?___

_(Translated from my original Korean essay)_

The author posits radical arguments throughout the book, particularly in Part 3, where he outlines his vision for the city's physical future. The pace of his speculative proposals often made me wonder if he was describing a city in the year 2200, as the ideas felt far removed from current reality. The following passage is a prime example:

“Why not remove height limits for the reconstructed apartments in Banpo and raise the floor area ratio to 600–700 percent, like Hong Kong’s Kowloon Peninsula? Then we could turn the remaining land into a dense park filled with trees so that people could bike in the shade even in summer. In other words, let’s set a goal to transform half of Seoul’s road and residential land into green space.”

He further argues that the additional profit gained through increased floor area ratios should fund youth housing developments elsewhere, prioritizing allocation by income level.

Because the author’s arguments are so idealistic, radical, and one-sided, I often felt compelled to push back. Typically, when reading a work by an expert, I try to be receptive and focus on learning. Yet this book was different. As the author hints in the preface, saying “I wonder how many readers will agree with my thoughts by the end of this book,” it becomes clear that his aim is not necessarily to persuade, but to state his position boldly. The title itself, *What’s Wrong with Apartments?*, signals a confrontational stance toward critics of apartment living. This led me to evaluate the author’s effectiveness as a debater throughout the text.

Upon closer reading, I realized that my discomfort stemmed from arguments that relied on "slippery slope" logic. The author frequently extrapolates sweeping conclusions from relatively minor observations without sufficient logical connection. For instance, in Parts 1 and 2, he cites cases like the transformation of the Gyeongui Line rail corridor into Yeontral Park, or Singapore’s connected canopy walkways, to show that engineered interventions can improve urban life. While these examples illustrate the benefits of deliberate design, they do not naturally lead to his much larger claims, such as building a bicycle highway to travel from Yangjae to Hannam Bridge in 15 minutes, or transforming Seoul into a high-density landscape reminiscent of Kowloon. Likewise, his proposal to convert half of Seoul into green space or extend the subway network to Line 20 so every home is within a five-minute walk feels disconnected from the earlier, more grounded examples.

In Part 3, the author’s main proposals fall into four categories: extremely high-density vertical development, undergrounding major transport infrastructure, expanding private-sector redevelopment, and adjusting presale and postsale systems. Collectively, this vision echoes Le Corbusier’s “Radiant City,” often criticized for its inhuman and unrealistic qualities. The author’s contemporary version appears to inherit similar flaws.

One major issue is the heavy reliance on private redevelopment. While private development can theoretically supply housing without public investment, in practice it operates strictly on profitability. Given that vacancy rates in non-metropolitan areas are twice those in the Seoul region, depending solely on private-led redevelopment could exacerbate the decline of regions where potential profits are low. Consequently, the author’s proposals raise significant concerns regarding real-world feasibility.

There are also valid concerns about the social implications of extreme apartment-centric development. High-density living increases the potential for noise complaints and neighbor disputes. Furthermore, gated open spaces reserved exclusively for residents can rigidify invisible social boundaries, fostering exclusionary environments.

The book’s structure also undermines its persuasiveness. As a compilation of previously published columns, it feels more like a collection of short essays than a cohesive thesis. Parts 1 and 2 discuss engineered improvements to daily life, Part 3 presents the author’s preferred urban model, and Part 4 discusses intangible urban values. The connection between these sections was not immediately clear. Even during our team discussions, we struggled to interpret the links, eventually categorizing Parts 1–2 as “tangible values” and Part 4 as “intangible values.” This lack of structural clarity weakened the impact of the author’s reasoning.

Despite these shortcomings, the book had a positive effect: it created a rare environment where our team felt free to question and debate. Because the arguments were overtly extreme and sometimes incomplete, they lowered the psychological barrier to expressing disagreement. My teammates quickly joined in as I pointed out inconsistencies, leading to a discussion on why the book felt “strange” and why the professor might have recommended it. In this way, the book was highly effective at generating conversation.

This may have been the author’s intention all along. He opens the book by recalling a debate from his school days, admitting he failed to articulate supporting evidence at the time. He concludes by suggesting, “Maybe everything in this book is a collection of the evidence I couldn’t present back then.” This implies that provoking debate may have been his primary goal. If so, he succeeded. His arguments prompted us to think critically about the future of cities and the values underpinning urban development.

Urban planning involves inherent value judgments. Stakeholder interests, minority opinions, and expert perspectives must all be weighed carefully. As we learned in class, some of history’s most visionary urban projects emerged from decisions that initially seemed unilateral. Because there is no single correct answer in city planning, critical thinking and active discussion are essential. This book, despite its flaws, sparked exactly that kind of discussion. And although the author’s proposals were extreme, he consistently maintained his stance, which I respect. He was not a subtle or tactical debater; rather, he confronted opposing views head-on. While this approach did not make his arguments entirely persuasive, it made the book a meaningful catalyst for conversation.

In that sense, I found value in reading *What’s Wrong with Apartments?*, not because I agreed with the author, but because the book encouraged us to articulate what kind of city we want to live in, and why.
