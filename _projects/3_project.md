# __Team Reflection on _What’s Wrong with Apartments?___


### __Overview__

Our team examined how Korea’s apartment-centered redevelopment model shapes the physical and social landscape of its cities. By looking at infrastructure projects, redevelopment patterns and everyday urban form, we explored the values behind large-scale construction and the trade-offs that come with it. Our goal was not to argue that apartments are inherently problematic but to understand what our built environment tells us about how cities in Korea are being shaped and for whom.
<br>

### __Background__

We began by questioning the idea that “artificial” or heavily engineered environments are automatically negative. Examples such as hydropower facilities and the restoration of Cheonggyecheon (page 6) showed us that intentional interventions can improve public space and support urban life. At the same time, we found clear evidence that infrastructure can bring risks and costs, from tunnel construction issues to worker fatalities during major bridge projects (pages 12–14). These cases helped us reflect on what values—efficiency, convenience, safety, or equity—are prioritized when cities develop.
<br>

### __Urban Form and Density__

To understand how different development choices shape land use, we created diagrams of dispersed low-density housing and high-density apartment clusters (pages 16–20). Through this comparison, we discussed how low-density layouts require more land and fragmented infrastructure, while high-density redevelopment concentrates residents but can produce uniform, monotonous urban landscapes. This exercise helped us think about how density, diversity and the human scale interact in the design of Korean cities.
<br>

### __Redevelopment and Its Implications__

We then analyzed how redevelopment takes place in Korea. Our review of examples on pages 21–25 highlighted that large-scale projects are often led by private stakeholders and tend to prioritize efficiency and economic return. While this model increases housing supply and can upgrade local infrastructure, it also accelerates land value increases and residential displacement. The charts on page 25 illustrate how redevelopment is closely tied to rising housing prices, reinforcing social inequalities and limiting who can remain in newly redeveloped areas.
<br>

### __Toward Better Cities__

In our conclusion, we argued that creating better cities requires more than building new structures. Cities should reflect values such as safety, environmental responsibility, inclusiveness and long-term resilience. We discussed how redevelopment needs to consider not only physical upgrading but also its impacts on communities, ecological systems and local identity.

Instead of asking “What’s wrong with apartments?”, we reframed the question as “What kind of urban environment should we aim for?” Through this project, we aimed to encourage a more balanced understanding of efficiency, social equity and environmental quality as we plan for better cities.

# __Personal Reflection on _What’s Wrong with Apartments?___

_(Translated from my original Korean essay)_

The author presents radical arguments throughout the book, especially in Part 3 where he outlines his vision for the physical direction of the city. The speed of his imaginative proposals often made me wonder whether he was describing a city of the year 2200, because the ideas felt far removed from current reality. The following passage is a clear example of this:

“Why not remove height limits for the reconstructed apartments in Banpo and raise the floor area ratio to 600–700 percent, like Hong Kong’s Kowloon Peninsula? Then we could turn the remaining land into a dense park filled with trees so that people could bike in the shade even in summer. In other words, let’s set a goal to transform half of Seoul’s road and residential land into green space.”

He goes on to argue that the additional profit gained through increased floor area ratios should be used to develop youth housing elsewhere, with priority given by income level.

Because the author’s arguments are so idealistic, radical and one-sided, I often felt an urge to actively oppose them. Normally when reading a work by an expert, I tend to be receptive and focus on what I can learn. Yet this book was different. As the author hints in the preface — “I wonder how many readers will agree with my thoughts by the end of this book” — it becomes clear that his aim is not to persuade but simply to state his position outright. The title What’s Wrong with Apartments? itself signals a confrontational tone toward critics of apartments. This made me want to assess how effectively the author played the role of a “debater” throughout the book.

As I reread the book more carefully, I realized that the discomfort I felt came from arguments that resembled a “slippery slope.” The author frequently makes claims in which relatively small observations are extended to sweeping conclusions without sufficient logical links. For example, in Parts 1 and 2, he cites cases such as the transformation of the Gyeongui Line rail corridor into Yeontral Park, or Singapore’s connected canopy walkways, to show that engineered interventions can improve urban life. These examples do illustrate that cities can benefit from deliberate design choices. However, they do not naturally lead to the author’s much larger claims — such as building a bicycle highway that lets people travel from Yangjae to Hannam Bridge in 15 minutes, or transforming Seoul into a high-rise, high-density landscape reminiscent of Kowloon. Likewise, his proposal to convert half of Seoul into green space or extend the subway network to Line 20 so every home is within a five-minute walk feels disconnected from the earlier examples.

In Part 3, the author’s main proposals fall into four categories: extremely high-density vertical development, undergrounding major transport infrastructure, expanding private-sector redevelopment, and adjusting presale and postsale systems. Altogether, this vision echoes Le Corbusier’s “Radiant City,” which was criticized for its inhuman and unrealistic qualities. The author’s contemporary version appears to inherit similar problems.

One issue is the heavy reliance on private redevelopment. While private development can theoretically supply housing without public investment, in practice it operates strictly according to profitability. As data show that vacancy rates in non-metropolitan areas are twice those in the Seoul region, depending solely on private-led redevelopment could deepen the decline of regions where profits are low. The author’s proposals therefore raise concerns about real-world feasibility.

There are also concerns about the social implications of extreme apartment-centric development. Dense high-rise living increases the likelihood of noise conflicts and neighbor disputes. Gated open spaces reserved for residents can make invisible social boundaries more rigid, fostering exclusionary environments.

Furthermore, the book’s structure contributes to its lack of persuasiveness. Because the book is a compilation of previously published columns, its organization feels more like a collection of short essays than a cohesive argument. Parts 1 and 2 discuss engineered improvements to daily life, Part 3 presents the author’s preferred urban model and Part 4 discusses intangible urban values. The link between these sections was not immediately clear. Even during our team discussions, we struggled to find a coherent interpretation and eventually categorized Parts 1–2 as “tangible values” and Part 4 as “intangible values.” The lack of clear structure weakened the persuasiveness of the author’s reasoning.

Despite these shortcomings, the book had a positive effect: it created a rare environment where our team felt free to question and debate. Because the arguments were overtly extreme and incomplete, they lowered the psychological barrier to expressing disagreement. My teammates quickly joined in as I pointed out inconsistencies, and we found ourselves discussing why the book felt “strange” and why the professor might have recommended it. The book effectively generated conversation.

Perhaps this was the author’s intention all along. He begins the book by recalling a debate from his school days, saying that he failed to articulate supporting evidence at the time. He concludes, “Maybe everything in this book is a collection of the evidence I couldn’t present back then.” This suggests that provoking debate may have been his goal. If so, he succeeded remarkably well. His arguments prompted us to think critically about the future of cities and the values behind urban development.

Urban planning inherently requires value judgments. Stakeholder interests, minority opinions and expert perspectives must all be weighed carefully. As we learned in class, some of history’s most visionary urban projects emerged from decisions that initially seemed unilateral. Because there is no single correct answer in city planning, critical thinking and active discussion are essential. This book, despite its flaws, sparked that discussion. And although the author’s proposals were extreme, he consistently maintained his stance, which I respect. He was not a subtle or tactical debater, but rather one who confronts opposing views head-on. While this did not make his arguments persuasive, it did make the book a meaningful catalyst for conversation.

In that sense, I found value in reading What’s Wrong with Apartments? — not because I agreed with the author, but because the book encouraged us to articulate what kind of city we want to live in, and why.
