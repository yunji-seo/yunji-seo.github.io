### __Background: Aging Society & Mobility Rights__

As of 2023, Korea is on the verge of becoming a super-aged society, with an aging rate of **18.4%**. However, the quality of life for the elderly remains a critical issue, evidenced by the highest elderly poverty rate (40.4%) and depression rate among OECD nations.

**Why Mobility Matters?**
For the elderly, the "Right to Move" is directly linked to social participation and quality of life. Research shows that independent mobility increases participation in leisure and cultural activities. However, low-income elderly populations, who rely heavily on public transit, are increasingly concentrated in **Permanent Rental Housing (영구임대주택)**.

* **Statistic:** Over 62% of single-person households in permanent rental housing are elderly (65+).
* **Problem:** Are these housing complexes located in areas that guarantee sufficient mobility for their residents?

<br>

### __Case Study: Large-scale Complexes in Nowon-gu__

We analyzed three major Permanent Rental Housing complexes in Nowon-gu, Seoul, supplied by LH (Korea Land and Housing Corporation) between 1989 and 1995.

**Findings on Accessibility:**
1.  **Seoul Junggye Jugong 3:** 12-minute walk to the nearest station (Sanggye Stn), hindered by slopes.
2.  **Wolgye Jugong 1:** 14-minute walk to Wolgye Stn. Poor overall access even with buses.
3.  **Junggye Jugong 9:** 18-minute walk to Hagye Stn. The route involves steep slopes without escalators, posing significant barriers for the elderly.

**Conclusion:** All three sites lack proximity to subway stations (12+ mins), severely restricting the mobility of elderly residents.
<br>

### __Policy Analysis: Why did this happen?__

The poor location of these housing complexes is a result of historical policy decisions and structural limitations.

**1. Mass Supply Strategy (1989-1995)**
To fulfill the "Two Million Housing Construction Plan," the government focused on rapid, large-scale supply. To minimize land acquisition costs for large complexes (1,000+ units), cheaper, less accessible land on the outskirts or hilly areas was selected over expensive transit-oriented plots.

**2. Centralized Supply System**
The centralized supply model by LH focuses on meeting quantity targets rather than addressing local needs. This "supply-driven" approach often ignores the specific locational requirements (e.g., flat terrain, transit access) of the elderly population.
<br>

### __Evaluation: Equity Perspective__

We evaluated the current policy based on **Equity Criteria**.

**1. Horizontal Equity (Failed)**
Residents in large-scale LH complexes pay similar rents but suffer from significantly worse accessibility compared to other public housing residents.

**2. Vertical Equity (Failed)**
Vertical equity suggests that those with greater needs (low-income elderly without cars) should receive better support (closer transit access). However, our comparison revealed the opposite:
* **Youth Housing (SH Happy Housing):** Located 5-10 mins from stations.
* **Elderly Housing (LH Permanent):** Located 12-18 mins from stations.

The current policy prioritizes convenience for the youth while marginalizing the elderly who physically need proximity the most.
<br>

### __Conclusion & Proposal__

To support "Active Aging," housing policy must shift from simply providing shelter to ensuring mobility and social connection.

**Proposed Solutions:**
1.  **Paradigm Shift:** Move towards a **"Compact City"** model (similar to Japan), placing elderly housing in transit-oriented zones to facilitate independent movement.
2.  **Local-Led Development:** Transition from centralized LH projects to local government-led initiatives.
    * *Benchmark:* **Dongjak-gu "Miso Housing"**. A mixed-use public housing project built on existing public land (daycare center site). By reducing land costs and understanding local context, it achieved both affordability and excellent accessibility.
