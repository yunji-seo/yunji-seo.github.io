Universal Redesign: Enhancing Accessibility in Pachuca, Hidalgo, Mexico

Spring 2025

This project was completed as part of “Community Planning and Public Policy for Disability” course at University of California, Berkeley.

Overview

Pachuca, the capital of Hidalgo, Mexico, is known for its rich mining history and cultural heritage. However, its historic urban fabric—characterized by steep terrain and cobblestone streets—creates significant barriers for people with disabilities. Focusing on Avenida Hidalgo, the city’s core artery, this project identifies physical and systemic exclusions faced by disabled residents and proposes a multi-pillar strategy (Legislative, Executive, Administrative, Participation, Attitudes) to foster an inclusive urban environment.

Problem Identification

[Fig. 1] Physical barriers on Avenida Hidalgo: Uneven cobblestones, lack of curb cuts, and narrow sidewalks force pedestrians onto the street.

1. Physical Barriers:

  • Terrain: Steep hills and uneven cobblestone surfaces make navigation difficult for wheelchair users and the elderly.
  • Infrastructure: Sidewalks are fragmented, narrow, and often blocked, lacking basic accessibility features like ramps or tactile paving.
  • Transit: The absence of an accessible public transportation system forces reliance on private vehicles or walking, further isolating those with mobility impairments.

2. Systemic Exclusion:

  • Data Gap: Over 31.9% of persons with disabilities in Mexico identify lack of accessibility as their main barrier, yet local data and consultation are often missing.
  • Social Exclusion: Physical barriers reinforce social invisibility, limiting participation in civic and cultural life.

Target Personas

To humanize the analysis, we developed personas representing the local demographic:

  • Felipe Estrada (41, Former Miner): Represents the working-age population with acquired disabilities (respiratory/mobility issues from mining) who are often marginalized after injury.
  • Lucia (65, Elderly Resident): Represents the aging population dealing with mobility decline, who struggles to access essential services like the church and market on Avenida Hidalgo.

Proposed Solutions: The 5-Pillar Approach

We proposed a holistic intervention strategy going beyond physical design.

1. Infrastructure & Transit Redesign

[Fig. 2] Proposed accessible minibus route connecting key destinations (Hospital, Market, Plaza) suitable for narrow streets, and service design.
  • Pedestrian Accessibility: Reconstruct sidewalks with smooth materials, install curb cuts at every intersection, and implement multisensory signage.
  • Public Transit: Introduce a fleet of accessible minibuses designed for narrow roads, operating on a fixed route connecting key nodes (e.g., Hospital, Market, Tourist Sites).
  • Transit App: Develop a reliability-focused app to build trust in the new system.

2. Policy & Administration (Pillars 1-3)

  • Legislative: Align local regulations with the National Law for Inclusion (2011) and UNCRPD standards.
  • Administrative Coordination: Establish a cross-agency “Real Del Monte Accessibility Task Force” to break silos between urban planning, transit, and social services.

3. Participation & Attitudes (Pillars 4-5)

[Fig. 3] Strategy for rebuilding trust: Direct engagement with disability advocacy groups and public forums.
  • Co-Creation: Directly involve disability advocacy groups (e.g., La Coalición Movilidad Segura) in the design process to address real needs and rebuild trust in public institutions.
  • Cultural Shift: Launch awareness campaigns to shift the narrative from charity to rights-based inclusion.

Conclusion

Inequality in urban access should not be accepted as a norm. By integrating physical upgrades with robust policy frameworks and community participation, Pachuca can transform its historic charm into an inclusive asset, ensuring that the “City for All” includes its most vulnerable citizens.
