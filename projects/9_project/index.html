Housing Complex Case Study & Model Making

Fall 2023

This project was completed as part of “Residential Areas Planning and Studio” course at Yonsei University.

Overview

This project explores the architectural evolution of high-density housing by comparing two distinct paradigms: the Shinonome Canal Court CODAN in Tokyo (an open, urban-integrated model) and the Olympic Athletes’ Village Apartments in Seoul (an isolated, self-contained model). Through 1:2000 scale modeling and hand-drawn plan analysis, our team investigated how physical layout influences social interaction and urban connectivity.

Background & Objective

As cities densify, the “warmth” of community and “vitality” of urban life are often lost in monolithic housing blocks. This study aims to identify design strategies that foster interaction.

We selected two case studies to contrast:

  1. Shinonome CODAN: A model of urban regeneration that overcomes the defects of former industrial sites to create a “downtown living” environment.
  2. Olympic Athletes’ Village: A monumental complex that, while innovative for its time, illustrates the issues of urban isolation and internal disconnection.

Case Study 1: Shinonome Canal Court CODAN (Tokyo)

“Connecting with the City”

View the Final Panel


1. Site Context & Concept Located in Koto-ku, Tokyo, this 16ha site was transformed from a factory district into a residential hub for approx. 2,000 households. The central concept was to create a space where work and life merge, utilizing a “Good Address, Activity, Variety” guideline to move beyond traditional dormitory-style housing.

[Fig. 1] 1:2000 Scale Model of Shinonome Canal Court CODAN
[Fig. 2] Shinonome CODAN Isometrics

2. The S-Shaped Avenue The most defining feature is the S-shaped pedestrian path winding through the six blocks.

  • Public Accessibility: Unlike gated communities, this path allows non-residents to freely access commercial and common facilities located on the ground floor.
  • Human Scale: Despite a high FAR of 400%, the S-road and low-rise decks mitigate the overwhelming scale of the 14-story towers, creating a comfortable walking environment.

3. Unit Variety & Transparency

Designed by six different architect teams, the complex offers 250 unit variations, including SOHO types. The design uses transparent and semi-transparent materials in common areas (like entryways) to encourage visual communication between neighbors, balancing privacy with community.

Case Study 2: Olympic Athletes’ Village (Seoul)

“Isolation and Disconnection”

View the Final Panel

1. Site Context & Design Built for the 1988 Olympics, this complex features a unique fan-shaped (radial) layout inspired by the flow of the Seongnae and Gami streams. It attempted to break the mold of south-facing orientation to prioritize views and skyline variety (6 to 24 stories).

[Fig. 3] Olympic Athletes' Village Isometrics

2. Critical Analysis: The “Island” Effect Despite its architectural significance, our analysis highlighted severe urban disconnection:

  • External Isolation: Located near the Greenbelt, the complex is surrounded by walls for privacy. This prevents neighbors from accessing natural amenities and forces pedestrians to take long detours, effectively creating a “locked” island.
  • Internal Disconnection: The radial shape results in vast distances between blocks (up to 1km), discouraging interaction.
  • Loss of Central Space: The “central courtyard” concept was compromised by surface parking, reducing the green space intended for community gathering.

Results & Conclusion

"How should high-density housing interact with the city?"

Through this comparative study and model making, we concluded that Shinonome CODAN offers a viable solution for future urban housing.

  • Openness vs. Closure: While the Olympic Village prioritized exclusivity through walls, Shinonome achieved security and community through “eyes on the street” and mixed-use active frontages.
  • Spatial Configuration: Shinonome’s S-road proves that high-density complexes can maintain a human scale and vitality if public and private spaces are carefully layered.

The project demonstrates that density does not have to mean “bleakness.” By designing for external connection and internal variety, urban housing can be a place of warmth and social exchange.
